# Spaceflight Experience and Medical Care (OCHMO-TB-033 Rev C)

> Fuente: https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/ochmo-tb-033-spaceflight-experience-medical-care-rev-c.pdf

NASA Office of the Chief Health & Medical Officer, NASA-STD-3001 Technical Brief, 25 July 2023. Extract of the sections on in-mission medical care. Derived from NASA-STD-3001 and for reference only.

## Relevant technical requirements

- **[V1 3003] In-Mission Preventive Health Care** — all programs shall provide training, in-mission capabilities and resources to monitor physiological and psychosocial well-being, based on an epidemiological evidence-based probabilistic risk assessment (PRA) that considers mission duration, expected return time to Earth, route and destination, radiation profile and concept of operations.
- **[V1 3004] In-Mission Medical Care** — all programs shall provide training, in-mission medical capabilities and resources to diagnose and treat potential medical conditions based on PRA, clinical practice guidelines and expertise, historical review, mission parameters and vehicle-derived limitations. "In-mission" covers all phases, from launch, through landing on a planetary body and surface activities, up to landing back on Earth.
- **[V2 7043] Medical Capability** — a medical system shall be provided to the crew to meet the medical requirements of NASA-STD-3001 Volume 1.

The knowledge and tasking of medical actions can be distributed among crewmembers, medical devices or aids, artificial intelligence systems, robotic systems, or similar. Incorporating ground medical support is key to many medical capabilities during flight.

## Expected conditions (all crewmembers to some degree)

- **Space Adaptation Syndrome** (first ~3 days): nausea, emesis, headache, malaise, vertigo; affects 50–70 % of crewmembers. Treatment: pharmaceutical options, inactivity, head movement exercises, head restraints, 1g orientation, pre-mission training/prophylaxis.
- **Head/nasal congestion** (space adaptation or CO2-related): affects > 50 %; leading cause of prolonged medication use in mission.
- **Fluid redistribution**: 12–15 % reduction in whole body volume, temporary diuresis.
- **Genitourinary issues**: a subset of crew experience urinary retention during space adaptation.
- **Space Adaptation Back Pain** (first 2 weeks): affects 52 %; positioning, exercises and medication (85 % relief).
- **Longer missions**: aerobic and muscle loss, bone loss ~1.5 %/month with renal stone risk, vision changes (SANS; 16 % show disc edema), altered jugular venous flow (6 of 11 crew; one occlusive internal jugular vein thrombus).
- Conditions treated differently in microgravity: **sleep loss** (sleep aids are the leading reason for acute medication use), **headaches** (elevated CO2, fluid shifts, pressure changes), **joint and muscle pain** (EVA and exercise equipment).

## Required in-mission capabilities (examples)

Administer and manage medications; assess and store vital signs (blood pressure, oxygen saturation, heart rate, respiratory rate); physical exam and screening; procedures (catheter, suturing, blood draw); behavioral health countermeasures; private medical/psychological conferences; sleep/fatigue management; evacuation; configure environment for care; physical or pharmaceutical restraints; **private two-way audio and video communication with ground**; **knowledge augmentation** (in-mission computer-based therapy, smart medical devices, just-in-time training).

Capabilities that may be needed for **medically autonomous missions (no real-time ground communications)** include individualized behavioral health countermeasures and evidence-based asynchronous behavioral health treatment protocols on electronic devices.

## Additional in-mission conditions for program consideration

Musculoskeletal (sprain, dislocation, fracture); traumatic injuries (back, neck, head, chest, abdominal; hypovolemic or neurogenic shock; barotrauma); ophthalmic (foreign body, chemical burn, corneal ulcer, retinal detachment, acute glaucoma, SANS); dental (caries, exposed pulp, abscess, crown loss); abdominal (hernia, nephrolithiasis, pancreatitis, cholecystitis, small bowel obstruction, appendicitis, diverticulitis, prostatitis); psychological/behavioral (burnout, depression, grief, anxiety, delirium, psychosis); infectious (pharyngitis, influenza, gastroenteritis, respiratory infection, herpes zoster reactivation); hearing loss; paresthesia; **decompression sickness secondary to EVA**; burns secondary to fire; **smoke inhalation**; **choking / obstructed airway**; **toxic exposure** (ammonia, hydrazine, methane, formaldehyde, Freon); sepsis; acute compartment syndrome.

At NASA, individuals with any substantial risk of significant medical illness are not certified for flight. If NASA-level selection is not used, major cardiovascular events (myocardial infarction, atrial fibrillation) and major neurologic events (seizures, stroke) may need to be considered for in-mission treatment.
